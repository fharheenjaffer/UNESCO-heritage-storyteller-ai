# Sun Temple, Konark

## Location
Konark, Odisha, India

## UNESCO Year
1984

## Historical Background
- The Konark Sun Temple was built in the mid-thirteenth century, traditionally dated to around 1250 CE, under the patronage of the Eastern Ganga dynasty king Narasimhadeva I.
- The temple was conceived as a monumental representation of the sun god Surya's celestial chariot, reflecting the dynasty's wealth and its devotion to solar worship.
- Over subsequent centuries the temple's main tower collapsed, likely due to a combination of structural issues, coastal weathering, and possibly damage during periods of regional conflict; the exact timeline of the collapse remains debated among historians.
- By the nineteenth century the site had become partly buried in sand, and colonial-era administrators undertook stabilization efforts, including filling the sanctum with sand to prevent further collapse of surviving structures.

## Architecture
The temple is designed as a colossal stone chariot for the sun god, featuring twelve pairs of intricately carved wheels, each over 3 metres in diameter, and originally drawn by a set of sculpted horses. Built from khondalite stone, the structure showcases extremely detailed relief carvings covering nearly every surface, depicting deities, dancers, musicians, animals, and scenes of daily and courtly life. The temple follows the Kalinga style of Odisha temple architecture, with the surviving audience hall (jagamohana) still standing prominently, though the original main sanctuary tower no longer survives intact.

## Interesting Facts
- The temple's 24 stone wheels are also sundials, precise enough that the time of day can be estimated from the shadow cast by their spokes.
- The structure is designed as a giant chariot pulled by seven sculpted horses, representing the days of the week.
- The temple's main tower is believed to have collapsed centuries ago; only the assembly hall (jagamohana) survives largely intact today.
- The site's intricate erotic and courtly sculptures, along with its chariot design, made it one of the inspirations behind the term 'Black Pagoda' used by European sailors navigating the coast.

## Story Version
Standing on the coastal sands of Odisha near the Bay of Bengal, the dramatic silhouette of the Konark Sun Temple rises before you like a mythical chariot carved from stone. Commissioned in the thirteenth century by King Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, this colossal monument was conceived as the celestial chariot of the sun god Surya. As you walk along its high terrace, you can examine twelve pairs of massive stone wheels, each over three metres across, sculpted with astonishing precision to function as astronomical sundials. Seven sculpted stone horses appear to gallop forward, pulling the giant chariot toward the morning sunrise. Every inch of the surviving khondalite assembly hall, the jagamohana, is adorned with intricate friezes of celestial dancers, musicians, warriors, and courtly life. Though European sailors once used its dark stone tower as a coastal navigational landmark called the Black Pagoda before the main sanctuary collapsed, standing here today fills you with awe at its ancient sculptural grandeur.
