# Taj Mahal

## Location
Agra, Uttar Pradesh, India

## UNESCO Year
1983

## Historical Background
- The Taj Mahal was commissioned by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan in 1632 as a mausoleum for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died during childbirth. Construction of the main structure took around two decades, and the surrounding complex, including gardens and outer buildings, was largely finished by 1653.
- The project brought together craftsmen and materials from across Mughal territory and beyond, including white marble from Rajasthan and semi-precious stones sourced from as far as Central Asia and the Arabian Peninsula. Contemporary chronicles describe thousands of workers, masons, and artisans engaged on the site.
- Shah Jahan himself was later confined by his son Aurangzeb to Agra Fort, from where he could view the Taj Mahal across the Yamuna River until his death. He was buried beside Mumtaz Mahal in the same mausoleum.
- In the centuries after Mughal decline, the monument suffered neglect and some damage during colonial-era administration, before a major restoration effort was undertaken in the early twentieth century under Lord Curzon, which helped stabilize the gardens and structure seen today.

## Architecture
The Taj Mahal is built almost entirely of white marble inlaid with semi-precious stones in a technique known as pietra dura, forming intricate floral and calligraphic patterns. The mausoleum sits on a raised marble plinth flanked by four minarets that lean slightly outward, a deliberate design choice so that in the event of collapse they would fall away from the main tomb. The structure blends Persian, Islamic, and Indian architectural styles, with a large central dome (an 'onion dome') and a symmetrical charbagh (four-part) garden layout representing the Islamic concept of paradise.

## Interesting Facts
- The Taj Mahal appears to change colour through the day, from pinkish in the morning to milky white in the evening, due to the way its marble reflects light.
- It is one of the New7Wonders of the World and one of the most visited monuments globally.
- The complex is perfectly symmetrical except for one element: Shah Jahan's own tomb, placed beside Mumtaz Mahal's, breaks the otherwise mirrored layout.
- A long-standing legend claims Shah Jahan planned a mirror-image 'Black Taj Mahal' across the river, though no solid archaeological evidence supports this.
- The marble used has a slight translucency, which is part of why it glows under moonlight, a feature that draws visitors to limited night viewings around the full moon.

## Story Version
As you step into the symmetrical charbagh garden layout beside the Yamuna River in Agra, the magnificent white marble silhouette of the Taj Mahal unfolds before your eyes. Commissioned in 1632 by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan as a mausoleum for his beloved wife Mumtaz Mahal, this monumental tribute brought together thousands of workers, masons, and craftsmen over two decades of construction. Walking along the raised marble plinth, you gaze up at the grand central onion dome flanked by four elegant minarets, deliberately tilted outward to protect the central tomb. Approaching the facade, you can marvel at the delicate pietra dura inlay work, where Rajasthan marble is seamlessly embedded with semi-precious stones forming intricate floral motifs and calligraphic patterns. In the soft shifting light, the translucent marble appears to change colour from morning pink to evening milky white, glowing with quiet grace. Looking south across the river toward Agra Fort, you can picture Shah Jahan in his final years of confinement, gazing across the water at the mirrored sanctuary where he would eventually rest beside Mumtaz Mahal.
