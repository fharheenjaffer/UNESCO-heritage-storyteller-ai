# Ellora Caves

## Location
Aurangabad district, Maharashtra, India

## UNESCO Year
1983

## Historical Background
- The Ellora Caves were carved over a long period from roughly the sixth to the eleventh century CE, under the patronage of successive dynasties including the Kalachuris, Chalukyas, and Rashtrakutas.
- Unlike Ajanta, which is exclusively Buddhist, Ellora represents a continuous history of religious coexistence, with Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain monastic and temple caves excavated side by side over centuries.
- The most ambitious undertaking at the site, the Kailasa Temple, was commissioned by the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I in the eighth century and is thought to have taken generations of workers to complete.
- The caves remained active sites of worship and pilgrimage for various communities well after their construction, and later became an important stop on regional trade and pilgrimage routes.

## Architecture
Ellora consists of 34 caves cut into a basalt cliff, comprising 12 Buddhist, 17 Hindu, and 5 Jain caves. The centrepiece, the Kailasa Temple (Cave 16), was carved top-down from a single rock outcrop, making it the largest monolithic rock excavation in the world, designed to resemble Mount Kailash, the mythological abode of Shiva. Its scale includes multi-storeyed gateways, courtyards, pillared halls, and elaborate sculptural panels depicting Hindu mythology. The Buddhist caves feature multi-storey viharas, while the Jain caves show more restrained but finely detailed carving.

## Interesting Facts
- The Kailasa Temple was carved by removing an estimated 200,000 tonnes of rock, entirely from the top downward rather than built up.
- Ellora is unusual for showing three religious traditions, Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain, built adjacent to one another over centuries.
- The site is located along a historic trade route between the Arabian Sea coast and the Deccan interior, which likely aided its patronage and prosperity.
- Some caves feature sophisticated multi-storey designs unusual among rock-cut structures elsewhere in India.

## Story Version
Walking along the Charanandri hills in Maharashtra, you are confronted by the astonishing sight of Ellora Caves, thirty-four rock-cut monuments spanning Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain traditions carved between the sixth and tenth centuries. You step into Cave 16, the monumental Kailasa Temple, and the scale takes your breath away: an entire multi-storey temple complex carved top-down from a single monolithic basalt cliff face under the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I. Walking through the colossal excavated courtyard, you realize that ancient stonecutters removed over two hundred thousand tonnes of volcanic rock using only hammers and chisels. Towering carved elephants, dynamic narrative friezes of the Ramayana and Mahabharata, and sculpted deities surround you at every level. Moving south to the Buddhist chaitya hall of Cave 10, with its arched ribbed ceiling, and north to the intricate Jain caves like Indra Sabha, you experience a remarkable testament to religious harmony and unmatched stone-cutting mastery that has endured for well over a thousand years.
