# Fatehpur Sikri

## Location
near Agra, Uttar Pradesh, India

## UNESCO Year
1986

## Historical Background
- Emperor Akbar founded Fatehpur Sikri in 1571 as a new capital city, reportedly in honour of the Sufi saint Salim Chishti, who had predicted the birth of Akbar's son and heir.
- The city was built rapidly, largely completed within about fifteen years, and served as the Mughal capital for roughly a decade.
- Despite the ambitious construction, the city was abandoned around 1585, most likely because of an inadequate water supply, and possibly also due to shifting military and political priorities toward the northwest frontier.
- After abandonment, the buildings survived in largely unaltered form because the site was never significantly redeveloped, leaving an unusually intact snapshot of Mughal urban planning.

## Architecture
Fatehpur Sikri is built almost entirely from red sandstone in a style that blends Persian, Islamic, and Hindu architectural traditions, reflecting Akbar's broader policy of cultural synthesis. Notable structures include the Buland Darwaza, one of the tallest gateways in the world, the Jama Masjid mosque, and the white marble tomb of Salim Chishti with its intricately carved lattice (jali) screens. Palace buildings such as the Panch Mahal and Diwan-i-Khas show inventive column arrangements and carved ornamentation drawing on Gujarati and Rajasthani temple architecture.

## Interesting Facts
- The city was the Mughal capital for only about ten to fifteen years before being deserted.
- The Buland Darwaza stands around 54 metres tall and was built to commemorate Akbar's military victory in Gujarat.
- Because the site was abandoned rather than continuously rebuilt, it preserves an unusually complete picture of a sixteenth-century Mughal city.
- Akbar used the city's Ibadat Khana (House of Worship) to host debates among scholars of different religions, an early experiment in interfaith dialogue.

## Story Version
Stepping onto the wide stone esplanade near Agra, the breathtaking Buland Darwaza looms fifty-four metres above you, a monumental triumphal gateway built by Emperor Akbar to celebrate his victory in Gujarat. Passing beneath its towering arch, you enter Fatehpur Sikri, the majestic red sandstone capital founded in 1571 to honour the Sufi saint Salim Chishti. Inside the vast courtyards, the silence feels profound across a city that was inhabited for only about a decade before being abandoned around 1585 due to scarce water supplies. You walk past the five-tiered colonnades of the Panch Mahal and the enigmatic central carved pillar of the Diwan-i-Khas, where Gujarati and Rajasthani motifs blend seamlessly with Islamic design. In the central courtyard of the Jama Masjid, the white marble tomb of Salim Chishti glows with delicately carved jali lattice screens. Here at the Ibadat Khana, Akbar once hosted groundbreaking interfaith debates among scholars of world religions, leaving behind an exceptionally preserved sixteen-century imperial city frozen in sandstone.
