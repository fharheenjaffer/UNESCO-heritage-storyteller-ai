# Qutub Minar and its Monuments

## Location
Mehrauli, Delhi, India

## UNESCO Year
1993

## Historical Background
- Construction of the Qutub Minar began around 1199 under Qutb-ud-din Aibak, the founder of the Delhi Sultanate, to commemorate the establishment of Muslim rule in Delhi. He completed only the first storey before his death.
- His successor Iltutmish added three more storeys, and the tower reached roughly its present height by the early thirteenth century. Later rulers, including Firoz Shah Tughlaq, carried out repairs after lightning damage.
- The surrounding complex grew over centuries and includes the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque, believed to be the earliest surviving mosque built by the Delhi Sultanate, constructed using materials and columns taken from earlier Hindu and Jain temples.
- The site also contains the Iron Pillar of Delhi, which predates the minar by many centuries and was likely moved to the location for its association with the older Hindu structures on the same ground.

## Architecture
The Qutub Minar is a tapering tower of red sandstone and marble standing about 73 metres tall, making it one of the tallest brick minarets in the world. It has five distinct storeys marked by projecting balconies, with fluted and angular ridges on the lower levels and intricate bands of Quranic inscriptions and geometric patterns carved into the surface. The complex around it mixes Islamic architectural elements with reused Hindu and Jain temple columns, giving it a hybrid stylistic character distinctive to early Delhi Sultanate architecture.

## Interesting Facts
- The nearby Iron Pillar has stood for over 1,600 years without significant rusting, a metallurgical feat still studied by scientists.
- The tower's five storeys were each built by a different ruler over roughly a century, giving it a slightly mismatched but historically layered appearance.
- The Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque was built using columns and materials salvaged from an estimated 27 demolished Hindu and Jain temples.
- Public access to climb the tower's interior staircase was closed after a stampede incident in 1981.

## Story Version
Standing at the base of the Qutub Minar in Mehrauli, you tilt your head back to gaze up seventy-three metres of fluted red sandstone and marble reaching into the Delhi sky. Commissioned around 1199 by Qutb-ud-din Aibak to mark the founding of the Delhi Sultanate, and expanded by his successor Iltutmish, this soaring minaret carries the marks of multiple rulers across five distinct projecting storeys. As you trace the bands of intricate Quranic inscriptions and geometric carvings winding upward, you feel the monumental scale of medieval Islamic architecture. Walking through the surrounding ruins, you enter the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque, where reused sandstone pillars carved with Hindu and Jain motifs reveal the remarkable hybrid artistry of early Sultanate masons. Just steps away in the open courtyard stands the ancient Iron Pillar of Delhi, unyielding and rust-free after more than sixteen centuries of weathering. The desert sandstone glows warm in the sunlight, inviting you to imagine centuries of scholars, muezzins, and travelers walking these historic grounds beneath one of the world's tallest brick minarets.
