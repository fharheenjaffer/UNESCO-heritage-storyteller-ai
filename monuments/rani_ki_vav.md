# Rani ki Vav (The Queen's Stepwell)

## Location
Patan, Gujarat, India

## UNESCO Year
2014

## Historical Background
- Rani ki Vav was built in the eleventh century, commissioned by Queen Udayamati in memory of her husband, King Bhima I of the Chaulukya (Solanki) dynasty.
- The stepwell served both a practical function, providing water access and a cool refuge during hot months, and a religious one, structured as an inverted temple honouring the sanctity of water.
- Over subsequent centuries, flooding from the nearby Saraswati River gradually buried the structure in silt, which paradoxically helped preserve its intricate carvings from weathering and damage.
- The stepwell was rediscovered and excavated by the Archaeological Survey of India in the 1980s, revealing sculptures in remarkably well-preserved condition.

## Architecture
Rani ki Vav is built as a seven-storey subterranean structure, unusually oriented east-west with descending stepped levels leading to a deep well shaft. Over 500 principal sculptures and more than a thousand minor ones adorn its walls, mostly depicting the ten avatars of Vishnu along with numerous other deities and celestial figures, arranged in a design intended to evoke the sanctity of a temple turned upside down to honour the importance of water.

## Interesting Facts
- The stepwell's design deliberately inverts temple architecture, treating water as the primary sacred element rather than a deity's image alone.
- Being buried under silt for centuries actually helped preserve its sculptures in exceptionally sharp detail.
- It is featured on the reverse side of India's 100-rupee currency note.
- The site contains over 500 major sculptures spread across its seven levels.

## Story Version
Approaching the quiet plains of Patan in Gujarat, the earth opens beneath you to reveal Rani ki Vav, a subterranean masterpiece descending into the earth like an inverted temple. Built in the eleventh century by Queen Udayamati as a memorial for King Bhima I of the Chaulukya dynasty, this grand stepwell served as both a practical water reservoir and a sacred architectural sanctuary. You descend seven levels of stepped terraces and pillared pavilions down toward the deep well, accompanied by the cooling embrace of underground air. Flanking your descent are more than eight hundred primary sculptures carved into the stone walls, depicting deities, celestial nymphs, and avatars of Vishnu, including a breathtaking carving of Sheshashayi Vishnu reclining on the multi-headed serpent. After centuries of silt accumulation from the nearby Saraswati River preserved these magnificent carvings in pristine condition until their twentieth-century excavation, walking down these ancient stone steps feels like descending into an underwater palace of sacred art.
