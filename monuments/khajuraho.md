# Khajuraho Group of Monuments

## Location
Khajuraho, Madhya Pradesh, India

## UNESCO Year
1986

## Historical Background
- The temples at Khajuraho were built primarily between roughly 950 and 1050 CE by rulers of the Chandela dynasty, who controlled the region during the medieval period.
- Originally the site is believed to have had around 85 temples spread over a large area; roughly 20 survive today, dedicated to both Hindu deities and Jain tirthankaras.
- The temples fell out of everyday use and were gradually enveloped by forest after the decline of Chandela power and subsequent political shifts in central India.
- The site was effectively rediscovered by a British engineer, T.S. Burt, in 1838, after which it drew growing scholarly and conservation attention through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

## Architecture
The Khajuraho temples are built primarily from sandstone in the Nagara style of North Indian temple architecture, characterised by tall curvilinear towers (shikharas) rising in clusters over the sanctum. The temples are renowned for extensive, detailed sculptural friezes covering their exterior walls, depicting deities, celestial beings, everyday life, and famously, intricate erotic sculptures that represent a small but widely noted portion of the overall carving. Interiors typically follow a sequence of halls (mandapas) leading to the sanctum, with elaborately carved ceilings and pillars.

## Interesting Facts
- Only about 20 of the original estimated 85 temples have survived to the present day.
- The erotic sculptures, though famous internationally, make up only around 10 percent of the total carvings at the site; most depict deities, animals, and daily life.
- The temples are grouped into western, eastern, and southern clusters, with the western group containing the largest and most visited temples, including Kandariya Mahadeva.
- The site remained largely hidden in dense forest for centuries before its rediscovery in the nineteenth century.

## Story Version
Walking along the manicured pathways of Khajuraho in Madhya Pradesh, the dramatic sandstone towers of medieval temples rise like forested mountain peaks against the horizon. Built between 950 and 1050 CE under the patronage of the Chandela dynasty, these surviving twenty sanctuaries out of an original eighty-five represent the pinnacle of northern Indian Nagara temple architecture. As you approach the towering shikhara of the Kandariya Mahadeva temple, you are surrounded by thousands of exquisitely detailed stone carvings wrapping around the exterior walls. Looking closely, you discover celestial nymphs, musicians, dancing deities, fearsome beasts, and everyday scenes of royal court life, alongside the famous erotic sculptural friezes that celebrate the fullness of human experience. Ascending the high plinth into the pillared mandapa halls, the scent of ancient stone and deeply carved ceilings guides you into the quiet sanctum. You can almost feel the centuries during which these architectural marvels lay hidden and protected within dense central Indian forests before their rediscovery in 1838.
