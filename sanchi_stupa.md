# Buddhist Monuments at Sanchi

## Location
Sanchi, Madhya Pradesh, India

## UNESCO Year
1989

## Historical Background
- The Great Stupa at Sanchi was originally commissioned by the Mauryan emperor Ashoka in the third century BCE, following his adoption of Buddhism after the Kalinga war.
- The structure was expanded significantly over subsequent centuries, particularly during the Shunga period, when the original brick stupa was enlarged and encased in stone, and elaborately carved gateways (toranas) were added around the second and first centuries BCE.
- Sanchi flourished as a Buddhist centre for roughly 1,300 years, with continuous additions of stupas, monasteries, and temples by different patrons, even as major Buddhist pilgrimage sites elsewhere declined.
- The site fell into disuse and obscurity after the decline of Buddhism in the region, and was rediscovered by a British officer, General Taylor, in 1818, after which extensive but sometimes damaging early excavation and restoration work took place.

## Architecture
The Great Stupa is a hemispherical dome (anda) built over relics, set on a raised terrace and enclosed by a carved stone railing (vedika), with four monumental carved gateways (toranas) at the cardinal directions depicting scenes from the Buddha's life and Jataka tales, notably without direct representation of the Buddha's human form, following early aniconic convention. The wider complex includes multiple smaller stupas, monastery ruins, and later temple structures, together illustrating the evolution of Buddhist architecture over more than a millennium.

## Interesting Facts
- The Buddha is never depicted directly in Sanchi's earliest carvings; his presence is instead symbolised through footprints, an empty throne, or a wheel, a convention typical of early Buddhist art.
- One of Ashoka's original stone pillars stands near the site, though it now lies broken.
- The stupa's four gateways are covered with some of the earliest surviving narrative stone reliefs in Indian art.
- Sanchi remained a place of active Buddhist worship and construction for roughly 1,300 years, longer than many other early Buddhist sites in India.

## Story Version
Ascending the quiet hilltop at Sanchi in Madhya Pradesh, the majestic hemispherical dome of the Great Stupa rises against the clear sky, surrounded by centuries of Buddhist devotion. Originally commissioned by the Mauryan emperor Ashoka in the third century BCE and later encased in stone during the Shunga period, this sacred mound was built to enshrine sacred relics of the Buddha. Walking along the circular stone terrace enclosed by the ancient railing, you approach the four monumental gateways, or toranas, set at the cardinal directions. You gaze up at the densely carved stone reliefs depicting Jataka tales and historical legends. In keeping with early Buddhist art tradition, the Buddha himself is never shown in human form, but rather through reverent symbols such as sacred footprints, an empty throne, or the wheel of the law. Walking past the ruins of monasteries, temples, and Ashoka's ancient pillar, you can feel the spiritual peace of a sanctuary that flourished continuously for over thirteen hundred years.
