# Elephanta Caves

## Location
Elephanta Island, near Mumbai, Maharashtra, India

## UNESCO Year
1987

## Historical Background
- The Elephanta Caves were carved primarily between the fifth and eighth centuries CE, most likely under the patronage of the Kalachuri dynasty, though the exact patrons remain debated among historians.
- The site was developed as a major centre of Shaivism (worship of the Hindu god Shiva), and its main cave contains some of the most celebrated sculptural representations of Shiva in Indian art.
- The island's Portuguese colonial name, 'Elephanta', comes from a large stone elephant sculpture that once stood near the shore; the sculpture later collapsed and was moved to a museum in Mumbai.
- Portuguese colonial forces caused deliberate damage to several sculptures at the site during the sixteenth century, reportedly using the caves for target practice, before the site later came under British and eventually Indian protection.

## Architecture
The main cave temple is cut into basalt rock and centres on a series of massive sculpted panels dedicated to Shiva, most famously the Sadashiva (Trimurti), a colossal three-faced bust representing Shiva's roles as creator, preserver, and destroyer. The cave follows a cross-axial plan with a central linga shrine, surrounded by large pillared halls and multiple subsidiary shrines. The carving style shows refined proportion and dramatic composition typical of mature rock-cut temple art in western India.

## Interesting Facts
- The island's name comes from a large stone elephant statue the Portuguese found near the shore, which is now displayed at a Mumbai museum.
- The Trimurti sculpture of Shiva, nearly 6 metres tall, is considered one of the masterpieces of Indian rock-cut sculpture.
- Portuguese soldiers used some sculptures at the site for firing practice, causing damage still visible today.
- The caves are accessible only by boat from Mumbai, adding to their relatively isolated, atmospheric setting.

## Story Version
Stepping off the ferry onto Elephanta Island in Mumbai harbour, a stone path through wooded hills leads you to the entrance of the ancient cave sanctuary. Carved into basalt rock between the fifth and eighth centuries CE under the Kalachuri dynasty, this sacred site was created as a magnificent shrine to Shiva. Entering the shadowy pillared hall of Cave 1, the cool underground air welcomes you as light filters across the massive rock-cut columns. At the far end of the hall, you stand face to face with the legendary Sadashiva, or Trimurti, a colossal six-metre-tall three-faced bust depicting Shiva's cosmic roles as creator, preserver, and destroyer. Walking through the cross-axial chamber toward the central linga shrine, you admire dramatic narrative relief panels carved with remarkable depth and proportion. Despite scars left by Portuguese soldiers who used the sculptures for target practice centuries ago, the profound spiritual majesty and artistic refinement of Elephanta remain deeply moving.
