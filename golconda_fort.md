# Golconda Fort

## Location
Hyderabad, Telangana, India

## UNESCO Year
null

## Historical Background
- The earliest fortification at Golconda dates to around the twelfth or thirteenth century under the Kakatiya dynasty, though the massive fort seen today took shape mainly under the Qutb Shahi dynasty, which ruled the region from the early sixteenth to late seventeenth century.
- Golconda served as the capital of the Qutb Shahi kingdom until the ruler Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah founded Hyderabad nearby in 1591, after which the fort continued to serve as a citadel and treasury rather than the primary royal residence.
- The fort and surrounding region became famous during this period as a centre of diamond trade, with several legendary stones, including the Koh-i-Noor, believed to have been mined and cut in mines associated with the Golconda kingdom.
- In 1687, after an extended siege, the fort fell to the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, ending Qutb Shahi rule and marking the beginning of the fort's gradual decline as a functioning citadel.

## Architecture
Golconda Fort is a massive granite hill fortress with outer walls stretching roughly 11 kilometres, incorporating multiple gates, bastions, and elaborate defensive engineering, including a sophisticated water-supply system that used Persian wheels to raise water to the upper levels of the fort. The fort is famous for its acoustic design at the main entrance, Fateh Darwaza, where a hand-clap at a specific point can reportedly be heard nearly a kilometre away at the highest pavilion, a feature that also served as an early-warning communication system. Within the walls stand royal apartments, mosques, armouries, and audience halls reflecting Qutb Shahi architectural style.

## Interesting Facts
- The fort's Fateh Darwaza entrance is engineered so a clap at its centre can be heard clearly at the Bala Hisar pavilion nearly a kilometre uphill, historically used to alert the royal court of arrivals.
- Golconda was historically one of the world's most important sources of diamonds, and gems such as the Koh-i-Noor and Hope Diamond are traditionally associated with mines near the region.
- The fort's water-supply system used Persian wheel technology to lift water to reservoirs at the highest points of the citadel, an advanced engineering feat for its time.
- After its fall to the Mughals in 1687, the fort was gradually abandoned as a capital, and much of Hyderabad's political centre had already shifted to the newly built city nearby.

## Story Version
Standing at the formidable Fateh Darwaza gateway of Golconda Fort in Hyderabad, you are surrounded by eleven kilometres of massive granite defensive walls climbing a steep rocky hill. Originally founded under the Kakatiya dynasty and dramatically expanded by the Qutb Shahi sultans during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, this citadel was renowned worldwide as a thriving centre of the diamond trade that yielded gems like the Koh-i-Noor. Stepping beneath the arched entrance, you clap your hands firmly at the central acoustic point; your clap echoes upward across the fortress, engineered so guards at the Bala Hisar pavilion nearly a kilometre uphill could hear the warning signal. As you climb stone stairways past armouries, royal palaces, and ancient Persian-wheel water reservoirs, the entire city of Hyderabad unfolds below. Imagining the eight-month Mughal siege led by Emperor Aurangzeb in 1687, you can feel the strategic brilliance and royal majesty embedded in every granite stone.
