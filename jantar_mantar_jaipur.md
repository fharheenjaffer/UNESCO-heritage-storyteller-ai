# Jantar Mantar, Jaipur

## Location
Jaipur, Rajasthan, India

## UNESCO Year
2010

## Historical Background
- The Jantar Mantar observatory in Jaipur was built between 1727 and 1734 by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II, a ruler with a strong personal interest in astronomy and mathematics.
- It was one of five similar astronomical observatories the Maharaja constructed across northern India, of which the Jaipur observatory is the largest and best preserved.
- Jai Singh drew on Hindu, Islamic, and European astronomical traditions of his time, sending scholars to study contemporary observatories and texts before designing his instruments.
- The observatory continued to be used for astronomical observation and calendar calculations for a considerable period after its construction, and it has undergone several restorations to keep its masonry instruments functional and accurate.

## Architecture
The site consists of 19 large masonry astronomical instruments, built from stone and marble, designed to measure time, track celestial positions, and predict eclipses with notable precision for the pre-telescope era. The centrepiece is the Vrihat Samrat Yantra, a giant sundial roughly 27 metres tall, claimed to be the largest stone sundial in the world, capable of telling time to an accuracy of about two seconds. Other instruments include devices for measuring the positions of stars, the sun's declination, and each of the twelve zodiac signs.

## Interesting Facts
- The Vrihat Samrat Yantra sundial can reportedly measure local time to within about two seconds of accuracy.
- Jaipur's observatory is the largest of the five similar observatories Jai Singh II built across northern India.
- The instruments combine Hindu, Islamic (Ptolemaic), and contemporaneous European astronomical knowledge.
- It remains a functioning observation site, with some instruments still used for basic demonstrations today.

## Story Version
Stepping into the open-air courtyard of Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, you enter a surreal landscape of geometric stone and marble instruments designed to track the cosmos. Commissioned around 1734 by the astronomer-king Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II, this astronomical observatory represents the pinnacle of pre-telescopic observational science in India. Walking among the nineteen large-scale instruments, you stand before the towering Vrihat Samrat Yantra, the world's largest stone sundial, rising twenty-seven metres above the ground. You watch in fascination as the sun casts a precise shadow across its sweeping curved marble quadrants, measuring local solar time with an astonishing accuracy of about two seconds. Nearby, brass astrolabes and zodiacal instruments align with celestial coordinates, designed so astronomers could observe planetary movements and eclipses directly with the naked eye. Surrounded by the terracotta walls of Jaipur, experiencing this UNESCO World Heritage Site reveals the extraordinary fusion of astronomical science, mathematics, and architectural vision created by eighteenth-century Indian scholars.
